# Game Integrity Lab

How do you test a model that flags players, when every mistake it makes accuses someone who did nothing wrong?

This repository is an evaluation testbed for game-integrity models. It generates synthetic poker players with known labels (legitimate players, bots, players using real-time assistance, colluding pairs), builds behavioural features, trains three kinds of model, and then spends most of its code on the evaluation: thresholds chosen under a false-positive budget, false-positive rates per player segment, robustness to players who adapt and to populations that drift, a regression gate, and checks on the evaluation harness itself, with seeded bugs that show which checks catch what.

It is a testbed and makes no claim to detect anything in the real world. Every player, session and decision here is synthetic, generated by `src/gil/simulate.py` from the parameters in `src/gil/config.py`. The models have never seen real data, and the numbers below describe how they behave on this simulation only. A statistical flag is never proof of cheating, and a false positive is a real person accused.

## Why this is a QA problem

A model that flags players is software whose output leads to decisions about people: a review, a frozen account, withheld winnings. Testing it is harder than testing most software, for reasons that are easy to lose sight of:

- **Positives are rare.** Around one player in nine is a positive in this synthetic population; in a real one the rate is unknown and probably much lower. A model that flags nobody is right almost every time, so accuracy says nothing. Precision, recall and PR-AUC do.
- **The two errors have different costs.** A missed bot costs the other players money. A wrongly flagged regular loses their account and their trust in the site. The operating threshold should come from an explicit budget for the second kind of error, set on data the final numbers are not measured on.
- **The unit is the player.** A bot plays thousands of decisions. Counting decisions instead of players inflates every metric, because the players with the most decisions dominate.
- **Averages hide who pays.** A 1% false-positive rate overall can mean 4% of strong regulars and nobody else. The report has to say which legitimate group gets flagged.
- **The opponent adapts and the population drifts.** A model tuned to today's bots is evaluated against tomorrow's.
- **The evaluation code can be wrong too.** A leaked label, a threshold tuned on the test set or a split that puts the same player in train and test all produce numbers that look better than the model is. Those bugs don't crash anything. They need their own tests.

## The data

A decision-level simulation, not a poker engine. Each player has sessions; each session has a sample of decision points. A decision point has a difficulty in [0, 1], three actions with EVs, and a reference ("solver-like") action distribution. Players deviate from the reference more on hard spots and late in long sessions, take longer on hard spots, and play at their own hours. Every assumption is written down in [`docs/data-model.md`](docs/data-model.md).

| Segment | Default count | Label | What makes it different in the generator |
|---|---|---|---|
| recreational | 1,400 | legitimate | Many mistakes, noisy timing, short sessions |
| regular | 900 | legitimate | Accurate on easy spots, much less on hard ones; slows down a lot on hard spots; fatigue; long sessions. 160 of them sit together as friend pairs |
| multi_tabler | 300 | legitimate | Fast, many decisions per hour, weaker link between time and difficulty |
| bot | 160 | flag (player task) | Almost no mistakes; fast timing with very low spread that ignores difficulty; 8 to 20 hour sessions at any hour |
| rta | 160 | flag (player task) | A regular who copies the reference on half of the hard spots and takes about 3 extra seconds when doing so |
| colluder | 50 pairs | flag (pair task) | Regular pairs who sit together in 70% of sessions and turn half their raises against each other into calls; 15 pairs also chip dump |

`gil generate --seed 42` gives 3,020 players and 1,393,393 decisions. The dataset has a fingerprint, printed in every report, so two reports can be checked to come from the same data.

## Features

Per player, in three groups (`src/gil/features.py`):

- **accuracy**: mean EV loss against the reference, on easy spots, on hard spots, the gap between them, agreement with the best action, and the fatigue gap (EV loss late in a session minus early);
- **timing**: mean log decision time, coefficient of variation, correlation between time and difficulty, extra time on hard spots;
- **schedule**: mean and longest session, spread of session start hours around the clock, decisions per hour.

Per pair of players who met in at least five sessions: shared sessions, share of their sessions spent together, decisions against each other, how often each raises the other compared with everyone else, and the extra EV each loses against the partner.

Features read only columns an operator's logs could hold. The generator's ground truth (segment, pair membership, why each action was taken) is kept in separate columns, and a check scrambles those and requires every feature to stay the same.

## Models

All three share one interface (a higher score means more suspicious) and are trained with fixed seeds (`src/gil/models.py`):

- **rules**: a transparent baseline. Each rule is a cutoff on one feature at the 2% tail of the legitimate training players (very regular timing, no link between time and difficulty, no worse on hard spots than on easy ones, very long sessions, play around the clock, near-reference accuracy). The score is how many rules fire, and the report prints every cutoff.
- **isolation_forest**: unsupervised, fitted on training players without their labels.
- **gradient_boosting**: supervised, scikit-learn's `GradientBoostingClassifier`.

The same three run on the pair task with pair rules (sit together often, one rarely raises the other, loses more against the partner).

## The evaluation

`src/gil/evaluate.py`, in order:

1. **Split by stake pool** into train, validation and test (60/20/20). Opponents come from the same pool, so every pair of players who met lands in one split and no player is in two.
2. **Fit** on train.
3. **Choose the threshold on validation**: the lowest score threshold that flags at most 1% of the legitimate validation players (`--budget`). Ties at the cut are left unflagged, so the budget holds exactly on validation. The same is done for a sweep of budgets (0.5%, 1%, 2%, 5%).
4. **Report on test**: PR-AUC, precision, recall, the confusion matrix, the false-positive rate with a 95% Wilson interval, and the flag rate for every segment, legitimate or not. Colluders are left out of the player task and their flag rate is reported separately.
5. **Robustness**: the frozen models and thresholds score four new populations: a fresh draw of the same generator, bots that adapt (deliberate mistakes, human-like timing and hours), more careful RTA users, and drift in the legitimate population (regulars who study and play longer, more multi-tablers).
6. **Report** as `report.json` (every number) and `report.md` (a readable view of the same numbers).

The **regression gate** (`gil gate baseline.json candidate.json`, `src/gil/gate.py`) compares two reports per task and per model: PR-AUC, recall and false-positive rate overall, the flag rate of every legitimate segment (must not rise by more than 1.5 points), the recall of every positive segment, and PR-AUC under each robustness scenario, with tolerances in [`configs/gate.json`](configs/gate.json). It refuses to compare reports computed on different data, and exits 1 when anything is out of tolerance.

## What the numbers show

Everything in this section is produced by `gil generate --seed 42` and `gil evaluate` (evaluation seed 7), on synthetic data. CI regenerates it on every push.

### Player task: bots and RTA users

581 test players, 65 of them positive (base rate 0.112).

| Model | PR-AUC | Precision | Recall | FPR on test (95% CI) | TP | FP | TN | FN |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rules | 0.585 | 0.971 | 0.523 | 0.19% (0.0% to 1.1%) | 34 | 1 | 515 | 31 |
| isolation_forest | 0.470 | 0.871 | 0.415 | 0.78% (0.3% to 2.0%) | 27 | 4 | 512 | 38 |
| gradient_boosting | 0.866 | 0.868 | 0.708 | 1.36% (0.7% to 2.8%) | 46 | 7 | 509 | 19 |

Flagged per segment at the 1% operating threshold:

| Segment | Legitimate | rules | isolation_forest | gradient_boosting |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| multi_tabler | yes | 0/61 | 0/61 | 0/61 |
| recreational | yes | 1/271 | 4/271 | 0/271 |
| regular | yes | 0/184 | 0/184 | 7/184 (3.8%) |
| bot | no | 34/34 | 27/34 | 34/34 |
| rta | no | 0/31 | 0/31 | 12/31 |

What this shows:

- **Gradient boosting is the best ranker and the one that flags regulars.** All seven of its false positives on test are strong regulars, 3.8% of that segment, while its overall false-positive rate is 1.36%. RTA users are simulated as regulars with help on hard spots, so the model that learns to find RTA also learns to suspect accurate regulars. A single overall rate would hide this.
- **The rules and Isolation Forest never catch an RTA user here.** Both find bots, which are unusual in several ways at once. RTA users look like good regulars on most features; only a supervised model that sees the accuracy-on-hard-spots pattern separates some of them.
- **Isolation Forest flags the players who are unusual, which is a different question from who cheats.** Its false positives are recreational players, whose play is noisy.
- **The budget held on validation and was exceeded on test** for gradient boosting (1.36% against 1%). With about 520 legitimate players in each split, the budget allows five flags on validation, and one or two more on a different set of players is ordinary sampling variation. The confidence interval (0.7% to 2.8%) says the same.

| player | isolation_forest | 0.280 to 0.481 | 0.078 to 0.483 | 0.78% to 2.30% | 3 of 7 |
| player | gradient_boosting | 0.843 to 0.907 | 0.673 to 0.758 | 0.57% to 2.33% | 5 of 7 |

| Model | recreational | regular | multi_tabler |

The pattern holds across splits: gradient boosting's false positives are almost all regulars, Isolation Forest's are mostly recreational players. The test false-positive rate of gradient boosting was over the 1% budget in five splits of seven, up to 2.33%. The PR-AUC range of one model across splits (0.843 to 0.907) is wider than the gate's tolerance (0.03), which is why the gate only compares reports computed on the same data with the same seed.

### Pair task: colluding pairs

1,246 candidate pairs met in at least five sessions; 290 are in the test split, 9 of them colluding.

| Model | PR-AUC | Precision | Recall | FPR on test | Friend pairs flagged |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| rules | 0.349 | 0.667 | 0.222 | 0.36% | 0/12 |
| isolation_forest | 0.819 | 0.438 | 0.778 | 3.20% | 5/12 |
| gradient_boosting | 0.892 | 0.889 | 0.889 | 0.36% | 0/12 |

Isolation Forest ranks colluders well and also flags five of the twelve friend pairs in test (18 of 114 over seven splits): friends sit together often, which is unusual, and the unsupervised model has no way to know that sitting together is legitimate. Nine positives on test is very few, so these pair numbers move a lot between splits (see the table above).

### Robustness: frozen models on new populations

| Scenario | Model | PR-AUC | Bots flagged | RTA flagged | Legitimate FPR | Most flagged legitimate segment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| fresh_population | rules | 0.570 | 160/160 | 0/160 | 0.65% | multi_tabler 1.0% |
| fresh_population | gradient_boosting | 0.885 | 160/160 | 53/160 | 0.92% | regular 2.7% |
| adaptive_bots | rules | 0.108 | 0/160 | 2/160 | 0.65% | multi_tabler 1.0% |
| adaptive_bots | isolation_forest | 0.078 | 0/160 | 0/160 | 1.00% | recreational 1.6% |
| adaptive_bots | gradient_boosting | 0.442 | 2/160 | 50/160 | 0.92% | regular 2.7% |
| careful_rta | gradient_boosting | 0.758 | 160/160 | 16/160 | 0.92% | regular 2.7% |
| legitimate_drift | rules | 0.557 | 160/160 | 0/160 | 8.88% | regular 24.6% |
| legitimate_drift | gradient_boosting | 0.623 | 160/160 | 56/160 | 16.35% | regular 47.0% |

(The full table with every model is in the report.)

- **Bots that add timing noise and deliberate mistakes get past every model.** Gradient boosting flags 2 of 160, the rules and Isolation Forest none. The bot signature the models learned is a set of habits a bot author can change.
- **RTA users who consult the tool less often are harder to see**: gradient boosting's RTA recall falls from 53 of 160 to 16 of 160.
- **Drift in the legitimate population is the worst case for innocent players.** When regulars study and make fewer mistakes, the frozen gradient boosting model flags 47% of them and the rules flag 25%, with thresholds that were within budget on the data they were chosen on. Recall hardly moves, so a dashboard that tracks only recall would show nothing wrong.

### The gate

CI runs the gate twice. A rerun of the same evaluation passes (57 comparisons). A candidate trained without the accuracy features fails with exit code 1; among its failures, gradient boosting's PR-AUC drops from 0.867 to 0.749, its RTA recall from 38.7% to 12.9%, and its flag rate on regulars rises from 3.8% to 9.2%.

## Checks on the evaluation harness

`validation/checks.py` holds 19 checks. Each one takes a `Pipeline` (`src/gil/pipeline.py`), the evaluation as a set of replaceable parts, so the same checks run against the real pipeline and against broken ones. Each names the kind of oracle it relies on.

| Kind | What it means here | Checks |
|---|---|---|
| Reference | Hand-computed small cases, and scikit-learn | Precision and recall by hand; PR-AUC by hand, with ties; the PR curve and PR-AUC against `sklearn.metrics` on 40 random cases with ties; the budget threshold by hand |
| Invariant | Must hold for any correct report | Every rate within [0, 1]; confusion counts add up to the evaluated players, and segment counts to the confusion; every chosen threshold keeps its budget on validation |
| Leakage | Nothing flows from labels or test data into features, training or thresholds | No player in two splits; features unchanged when every ground-truth column is scrambled; shuffling test labels leaves every threshold unchanged; no reported unit was trained on |
| Sanity | A model that can know nothing must not look good | With labels permuted over the whole dataset, supervised PR-AUC falls to about the base rate; flagging nobody cannot beat the base rate on the headline metric; every model beats chance on validation |
| Metamorphic | A change to the input with a known effect on the output | Row order changes nothing; duplicating the dataset changes no rate and no threshold; a strictly increasing transform of every feature leaves gradient boosting's training scores unchanged (tree splits depend only on order), and a positive affine one leaves Isolation Forest's unchanged |
| Determinism | Same seed, same result | Two runs with the same seed give identical player-task reports; the generator gives the same data for the same seed and different data for a different one |

`tests/test_metrics.py` adds [Hypothesis](https://hypothesis.readthedocs.io/) properties: PR-AUC, precision and recall match scikit-learn on generated inputs with many ties; the PR curve has one point per distinct score with recall rising to 1; the budget threshold keeps the budget and is the lowest threshold that does; moving positive scores does not move it; a monotone transform of the scores leaves PR-AUC unchanged. `tests/test_simulate.py` checks that each population has its intended signature in the features.

## Testing the tests: seeded bugs

`validation/mutants.py` holds eleven versions of the pipeline with one deliberate bug each, all of them mistakes that are easy to make when evaluating a model that flags players. `scripts/run_seeded_bugs.py` runs every check against every one of them and prints this table (quarter-size population, seed 11):

| Seeded bug | Stage | GB PR-AUC | IF PR-AUC | GB reported | Caught by |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| none (real pipeline) | | 0.853 | 0.694 | precision 0.83, recall 0.75 | nothing (passes every check) |
| Players leak across splits (split by session) | split | 1.000 | 0.507 | precision 0.91, recall 1.00 | `no_player_in_two_splits` (leakage), `threshold_ignores_test_labels` (leakage), `reported_units_were_not_trained_on` (leakage), `shuffled_labels_fall_to_base_rate` (sanity) |
| Split ignores its seed (shared global RNG) | split | 0.693 | 0.293 | precision 0.64, recall 0.50 | `threshold_ignores_test_labels` (leakage), `same_seed_same_report` (determinism) |
| Feature built from a ground-truth column | features | 1.000 | 0.868 | precision 1.00, recall 1.00 | `features_ignore_ground_truth` (leakage) |
| Isolation Forest score sign not flipped | model | 0.853 | 0.089 | precision 0.83, recall 0.75 | `every_model_beats_chance` (sanity) |
| Threshold tuned on the test set | threshold | 0.853 | 0.694 | precision 0.93, recall 0.70 | `thresholds_keep_the_budget_on_validation` (invariant), `threshold_ignores_test_labels` (leakage) |
| FP budget computed over all players | threshold | 0.853 | 0.694 | precision 0.83, recall 0.75 | `threshold_by_hand` (reference), `thresholds_keep_the_budget_on_validation` (invariant) |
| Evaluated on the training players | evaluation | 1.000 | 0.476 | precision 1.00, recall 1.00 | `reported_units_were_not_trained_on` (leakage), `shuffled_labels_fall_to_base_rate` (sanity) |
| Metrics over decisions, not players | evaluation | 0.960 | 0.900 | precision 0.95, recall 0.88 | `counts_add_up` (invariant) |
| Precision and recall swapped | metrics | 0.853 | 0.694 | precision 0.75, recall 0.83 | `precision_recall_by_hand` (reference) |
| PR curve drops its last point | metrics | 0.853 | 0.694 | precision 0.83, recall 0.75 | `pr_auc_by_hand` (reference), `pr_curve_matches_sklearn` (reference) |
| Accuracy as the headline metric | report | 0.853 | 0.694 | accuracy 0.95 as the headline | `flagging_nobody_scores_nothing` (sanity) |

All eleven are caught, and the real pipeline passes all 19 checks. The tests assert both, and CI writes the table to the job summary.

What the table says:

- **The most dangerous bugs make the model look better.** Leaking players across splits, a label-derived feature and evaluating on training players all report a PR-AUC of 1.000 for gradient boosting against 0.853 for the real pipeline. Nobody would question those numbers from the report alone; the leakage checks exist because the report can't reveal its own leak.
- **Some bugs don't change this run's numbers at all.** Computing the budget over all players instead of legitimate ones allows the same number of false positives here, because the positives are too few to change the rounding; the PR curve bug happens not to move PR-AUC on this data. Both are still wrong, and only the hand-computed reference cases see them reliably. Judging an evaluation by whether its numbers look plausible would pass both.
- **One bug is one check away from going unnoticed.** Counting decisions instead of players is caught by a single invariant (the confusion matrix counts more units than there are players). It raises the reported precision from 0.83 to 0.95.
- **Sanity checks catch what reference checks can't.** The unflipped Isolation Forest sign is correct code with the wrong meaning; only "every model must beat chance" notices. Accuracy as the headline is a correct number that answers the wrong question; only "flagging nobody must not look good" notices.
- **Some checks catch none of these bugs.** The metamorphic checks, the range invariant and the generator's determinism are not needed for this table. They guard against mistakes this table has no seeded bug for (a metric that depends on row order or on the size of the dataset, a rate outside [0, 1], a generator that ignores its seed). A table like this measures the checks against the bugs someone thought of, and says nothing about the rest.

## Running it

```bash
pip install -e ".[dev]"
pytest -q                                   # about a minute: checks, properties, seeded bugs, gate, CLI

gil generate --out data --seed 42           # synthetic players, sessions and decisions
gil evaluate --data data --out out/baseline # report.json and report.md
gil evaluate --data data --out out/candidate --exclude accuracy
gil gate out/baseline/report.json out/candidate/report.json --config configs/gate.json --markdown gate.md

PYTHONPATH=src:. python scripts/run_seeded_bugs.py   # the seeded-bugs table
PYTHONPATH=src:. python scripts/split_spread.py      # spread across evaluation seeds
```

`python -m gil ...` works the same as `gil ...`. Other options: `gil generate --config small` (a quarter of the population), `--scenario adaptive_bots` (or any other scenario), `gil evaluate --budget 0.005`, `--no-scenarios`, `--no-pairs`, `gil gate --all` to list every comparison. Exit codes: 0 when the gate passes, 1 when it fails, 2 when an input can't be read or the reports can't be compared. Python 3.11 or later, with numpy and scikit-learn.

## Limitations

- **All data is synthetic, and the results follow from the generator.** The populations were designed to have the signatures the features look for; real bots, RTA users and colluders are not obliged to. The numbers above show how the evaluation behaves, and nothing about how well these features or models would work on a real site.
- The generator is given the difficulty of every spot and the reference action. Real systems would estimate both, with errors of their own, and RTA users would copy a tool that does not agree exactly with the reference used to judge them.
- The test split holds 31 RTA users, 34 bots and 9 colluding pairs. Per-segment rates on that many units have wide intervals, which the report prints for the overall false-positive rate only.
- Labels here are complete and correct. Real labels come from investigations of players who were already flagged, so they are biased towards what earlier models could see, and the legitimate class contains cheaters nobody caught.
- The pair task scores only pairs who met in at least five sessions, and a pair whose members fall into different splits is not evaluated.
- The models are fitted once, with fixed hyperparameters and no tuning. The subject of this repository is how model quality is measured, so the models are deliberately plain.

## Next

- Seeded bugs for the checks that catch nothing yet, such as a metric that depends on row order or a generator that ignores its seed.
- Intervals for every per-segment rate, and a gate that compares intervals instead of point values.
- Label noise and selection bias in the training labels, to measure how an evaluation behaves when its ground truth is itself the output of earlier flags.
